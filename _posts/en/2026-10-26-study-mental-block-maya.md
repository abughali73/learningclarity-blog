A study mental block can feel like this: you know every word in a sentence, but cannot explain the idea without looking. Maya has reached that point. She has read the passage seven times, yet correlation and causation still blur together when she tries to explain them.

Our short film follows this fictional student through one concept, using real LearningClarity app demonstrations. Watch the full story, then try the sequence with a difficult passage of your own.

## 1. Make the study mental block a specific question

Maya does not begin with a request to explain an entire subject. She returns to one confusing passage from her seminar material and asks what it actually means. That gives her a smaller task: identify the relationship she cannot yet explain.

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

In **Explain Mode**, LearningClarity teaches a subtopic one short section at a time. You can ask your own question about a section or request an example. Instead of repeating “I don’t understand”, try asking: “What does this sentence claim, and which part of that claim am I unsure about?”

Write down the question before you continue. It gives you something concrete to check when you return to the source.

## 2. Use Simplify for the language, Visualize for the relationship

**Simplify** rephrases the current section in simpler terms. In Maya’s session, clearer wording offers another way into the difference between two ideas. The purpose is to revisit the concept, then compare the simpler explanation with the original passage.

Next, she chooses **Visualize**. This generates a clean, labelled diagram of the current concept in Explain Mode. In the film, the actual diagram places correlation and causation side by side. The narrator explains the feature while Maya examines the comparison before returning to her example.

The café example asks what observations about coffee, cold drinks and outdoor study can support. Things occurring together do not, on that evidence alone, establish that one caused another. Maya now has a more focused question: what evidence would be needed for the stronger conclusion?

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

Try the same comparison. First, state the simpler explanation. Then point to the relevant parts of the diagram. Finally, explain how those parts relate to the original sentence. For another practical approach, see our [guide to studying with labelled diagrams](/en/2026/10/visual-learning-with-ai-diagrams/).

## 3. Let the question test your explanation

A familiar-looking diagram is another starting point, not a reason to stop checking. Maya encounters a knowledge check, notices a mistaken assumption and returns to the reasoning.

LearningClarity’s **Knowledge Checks** are multiple-choice questions during the explanation, with feedback and **Retry**. **Quiz Mode** offers focused practice using selected subtopics and question types. In the story, Maya also records a note in her own words and tries explaining the idea away from the screen.

Close your notes and try this: give a definition, add an example, and name one question you still cannot answer. Return to that gap rather than rereading everything from the beginning.
