The test came back face-down on a grey Thursday morning. Sofia turned it over in the middle row of the lecture hall and read the number twice: 61%. All week her practice quizzes had said 85%. Sofia, her lecturer and her university are fictional, but the gap between practice quizzes and a real test is one many students know.

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

Before she reached the door, Dr. Okafor, her cell biology lecturer, said the line she was dreading: "Unit two test in three weeks. Anyone under 50% across both tests repeats the module in summer."

## The myth: a lucky guess still counts

Sofia knew exactly what she had been doing. On every practice quiz, when she wasn't sure, she guessed. With four options she was often lucky, and the score looked good. Her belief was simple: a high practice score means I know it.

The test had just proved her wrong. Rereading her notes three more times would not tell her which points she understood and which ones she had only guessed right.

## Sunday: practice quizzes with one rule

On Sunday evening she opened [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai) and typed: "Cell biology for first-year university students, unit two: membrane transport, cell signalling and the cell cycle." LearningClarity built the topic and generated its subtopics.

Then she opened **Quiz Mode**, chose the subtopics and the question types, and wrote one rule on a sticky note: no guessing. Whenever she wasn't sure, she pressed **"I don't know"**.

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

It felt strange. Her finger hovered over an answer that looked about right, and she pressed "I don't know" instead.

## Monday: the honest score that stung

The result was 52%. The screen showed her score, a breakdown of correct, incorrect and "I don't know" answers, and a mastery level lower than she wanted to see.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

For an hour it felt like going backwards. Then she looked at where the "I don't know" answers sat. They were not spread everywhere. Almost all of them were in two subtopics: active transport and cell signalling. For the first time, her practice score was telling the truth, and the truth was useful.

## Two weeks of studying only the gaps

Instead of rereading everything, Sofia opened **Explain Mode** on those two subtopics, which taught her one short section at a time. When active transport turned into a blur of pumps and gradients, she tapped **Simplify**, and the section came back in plainer words. On the hardest concept she used **Visualize** to get a clean, labelled diagram, then drew her own version in her notebook. (Maya used the same pair to break through a mental block: here is [how Simplify and Visualize work together](/en/2026/10/study-mental-block-maya/).)

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

After each subtopic she went back to Quiz Mode, with the same rule. When she got a question wrong, she asked why the answer was incorrect and copied the reason into her notes. Her scores climbed slowly: 58%, then 66%, then 71%. Lower than the old 85%, but every point was real.

## Three weeks later: a test that matched

The night before the unit two test, her last practice quiz came in at 72%, with only two "I don't know" answers left.

Two days later the paper came back face-down again. She turned it over in the corridor: 74%.

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

The grade was her own work, built over three weeks of honest practice. What struck her most was that, for the first time, the result was no surprise. Her lab partner leaned over to look. "How did you know you'd do well?" he asked. "I stopped lying to myself on practice quizzes," Sofia said.

## Three takeaways from Sofia's story

- **A guess hides a gap.** An honest "I don't know" shows you exactly where to study next.
- **A lower score can be better news.** A score you can trust beats a score that flatters you.
- **Study the gaps, not the whole book.** Let your quiz results tell you which subtopics need you.
