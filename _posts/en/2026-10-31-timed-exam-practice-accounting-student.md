Question 27 of 40, and the invigilator said, "Ten minutes left." Youssef, a second-year accounting student who had skipped timed exam practice all term, looked at a consolidation question he knew how to solve, then at the thirteen he hadn't read yet. He wrote fast, guessed the rest and walked out of the midterm knowing the material, and knowing it hadn't shown.

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

## Before: the paper that ran out

Two days later his lecturer, Dr. Lindqvist, slid the marked paper across her desk. "Everything you answered is right," she said. "But you answered 27 questions. The final is in five weeks, same format: 40 questions, 90 minutes. The audit firm's internship offer depends on you passing this module. Be faster."

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

Youssef, Dr. Lindqvist and the audit firm are fictional, but plenty of students will recognise the problem. Rereading his notes wouldn't help; he already understood them. Another textbook wouldn't help either. What he had never done was practise under the conditions he would be tested in.

## Week one: timed exam practice on Hard

That Sunday he exported his lecture notes to PDF and uploaded them to [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai). It built a topic and its subtopics from the content: accruals, depreciation, inventory valuation, consolidation and cash flow statements.

Then he opened Exam Mode and set it up like the real thing: the whole topic as the scope, Hard difficulty, timed, 30 questions. No pausing, no notes.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

It went badly. Time ran out with questions still open, and the score was barely more than half. For a minute he thought about closing the laptop for the night.

Instead he opened the results. The full question review showed every answer, and the AI performance summary listed his strengths and his areas to revisit. Once it was in front of him, the pattern was obvious: journal entries and depreciation were solid, but he lost minutes on long consolidation questions, working them out from scratch and then second-guessing himself.

## Weeks two to four: review every wrong answer

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

Youssef made one rule: no new mock exam until every wrong answer from the last one made sense. For each one he opened the subtopic in Explain Mode, tapped Simplify when the wording tangled, Explain Further when he wanted more detail, and asked for an example with figures.

Midweek, on the bus to campus, he ran short Quiz Mode sessions on consolidation only. He pressed "I don't know" instead of guessing, so his results showed what he really knew, and he asked why each answer was right. (If guessing is your habit too, read [why practice quizzes can fool you](/en/2026/10/practice-quizzes-stop-guessing/).)

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

The reviews also changed how he worked through a paper, and that part was his own doing. Short questions first. Long consolidation questions marked and saved for a second pass. Every Sunday, another timed mock on Hard; in the final week he switched to Random difficulty so nothing felt predictable. Each week, fewer questions were left open at the end.

## After: question 27 of 40, again

Same hall, same invigilator. "Ten minutes left." This time Youssef was on question 38. He finished, went back, and had time to check the two answers he had marked as doubtful. He changed one of them.

{% include scene.html n=6 %}

When the results came out, Dr. Lindqvist stopped him in the corridor. "That's the paper I knew you could write," she said. The internship email arrived a week later. The pass was his, earned in a hall with a clock on the wall; the mock exam reviews had shown him which questions to tackle first.

## Three takeaways

1. Practise under the conditions you'll be tested in, clock included.
2. A wrong answer only helps once you understand it, so review before the next attempt.
3. Speed comes from knowing your weak question types, not from reading faster.

Set up a timed exam on your topic this week, and review every question afterwards.
