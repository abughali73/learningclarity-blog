On the first Sunday of term, the kitchen table disappeared under paper. Elif had 400 slides of embryology. Mateo had a data structures module with a midterm in seven weeks. Hannah had a contract law reader as thick as a brick. Three flatmates, three majors, one shared problem: they needed a study strategy for university that didn't start with weeks of typing flashcards. Elif, Mateo, Hannah and their university are fictional.

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

## Elif, medicine: from slides to a sequence

Elif exported her lecture slides to PDF, because PowerPoint decks can't be uploaded directly, and uploaded them to [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai). It built a topic and its subtopics from the content, so the order of her own lectures became her study path.

Development by weeks was where she got lost. When the neural tube closure and the looping heart blurred together, she opened the subtopic in Explain Mode and tapped Visualize. A clean, labelled diagram of that one concept appeared. A Common mistake callout pointed out the trap her lecturer loved. She went through it one short section at a time, and tapped Simplify when a paragraph was too dense.

## Mateo, computer science: recursion, one frame at a time

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

Mateo uploaded his lecture notes and asked the question he was embarrassed to ask in class: "Why does the function finish in reverse order?" Explain Mode answered that section, and Visualize drew the call stack as a labelled diagram. He still wrote and debugged every line of his own code; the platform helped him understand why it worked.

Then he opened Quiz Mode and chose all four question types: multiple choice, true/false, fill in the blank and open-ended. The open-ended questions hurt. He pressed "I don't know" twice instead of guessing, and asked why the right answers were right.

## Hannah, law: the reader that wouldn't upload

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

Hannah's setback came first. Her photocopied reader was a scanned PDF without a text layer, and scanned PDFs like that can't be read. She found the text versions of the cases in the library database, saved them as PDFs and tried again. This time the topic and subtopics appeared.

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

Her strategy was time. On the tram she used Listen to hear each explanation read aloud. For the long weekend trains home, she turned the contract formation subtopic into an AI podcast: a two-voice conversation between a host and an expert guest. (For more on this, see [how to study your own PDFs](/en/2026/10/study-your-own-pdfs-with-ai/).)

## Midterm week: the budget and the kitchen table

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

None of them wanted another subscription. They started with the 300 free credits each of them got with a new account, then bought a credit pack when they needed one. Packs start at $1.99 for 1,000 credits, and credits never expire, so nothing was wasted between exam periods.

Mateo shared his recursion topic with Elif by email after she admitted she'd never understood it. She accepted and got her own independent copy with her own progress.

{% include scene.html n=6 %}

On the last day of exams they walked out into the cold sun together. "Same table in January?" Hannah asked. Elif laughed. "Same table. Fewer slides."

**Three takeaways:**

1. Start from the material your course actually uses, not a blank page.
2. Match the feature to the moment: diagrams at the desk, audio on the move.
3. Press "I don't know" when you aren't sure; it shows you where to study next.

Upload one lecture you dread, and take a short quiz on it before you reread a single slide.
