Fifteen minutes. That was the deal Aisha made with herself on the first of the month, after the children were finally asleep. A daily study habit that small felt almost silly, but it was the only kind she could keep, and she had exactly thirty days.

That afternoon Helen, her manager, had put a thick folder on her desk. "I retire at the end of the month," she said. "From next month, the stock report is yours. First one is due on the first working day."

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

Aisha was good at her job. She coordinated deliveries for a furniture wholesaler, knew every driver by name. But Helen's monthly report meant conditional sums, lookups between sheets, pivot tables and charts the directors could read at a glance. Aisha could type numbers into a spreadsheet. She could not make one answer a question. (Aisha, Helen and the company are fictional.)

## Day 1: a daily study habit, fifteen minutes long

A weekend course was out of the question with two children and a full-time job, and the hour-long video tutorials she had bookmarked never got watched. So at 9 PM she opened [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai) and typed her situation as a topic description: "Spreadsheets for a delivery coordinator who has to build a monthly stock report: conditional sums, lookups, pivot tables and simple charts. I'm a beginner with formulas."

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

LearningClarity built the topic and generated its subtopics, and she chose to focus on the four her report depended on. Then she set a kitchen timer. **Explain Mode** taught one short section at a time, and she selected Next only when the last one made sense. When conditional sums sounded like a riddle, she tapped **Simplify** and got the same idea in plain words. Fifteen minutes later the timer rang, and she stopped. The **First Topic** achievement was already waiting for her.

## Day 7: the badge, and a reason to keep going

The streak became the spine of her evenings. Her **current streak** counted the consecutive days she studied, and she switched on the optional streak reminder so her phone would nudge her before bed. On day 7 the **7-Day Streak** achievement appeared. Small, but it made her smile.

## Day 9: the night the streak broke

Then came the school project. Her son's cardboard volcano needed glue, paint and a mother until 11 PM, and Aisha fell asleep on the sofa without opening anything. The next morning her current streak was back to zero.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

She nearly gave up. What kept her going was the other number: her **longest streak** still said 8, her personal best. Nothing had been lost, and **Resume** took her straight back to where she had stopped. So she changed the rule. On a bad day, the deal was no longer fifteen minutes. It was one short section and its **Knowledge Check**. That was all.

## Days 10 to 29: small enough to keep

There were plenty of bad days. On those nights she sat in the hallway outside the children's room, did one section on her phone and answered its Knowledge Check. Her library and progress were the same on every device, so the next evening she carried on at the laptop. When she got a question wrong, the feedback told her why, and **Retry** let her try again.

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

Pivot tables were the wall. Rows, columns and values blurred together until one Saturday morning she tapped **Visualize**, and a clean, labelled diagram showed how the pieces fitted. She copied it into her notebook while her son ate cereal and asked what the boxes were for. {% include scene.html n=5 %}

## Day 30: the report

On the last morning of the month, Aisha built the stock report herself, with real numbers from real deliveries. The conditional sums added up and the pivot table answered the directors' usual question in one view. Her current streak read 21, not 30, and she was fine with that. The report was her work; the evenings had given her the skills to do it.

{% include scene.html n=6 %}

Helen turned the pages slowly. "I spent years building this," she said. "You built it in a morning."

## Three takeaways

- **Make the daily step small enough to keep.** Fifteen minutes on a good day, one section on a bad one.
- **A broken streak is information, not failure.** Your longest streak keeps your best, and your progress stays put.
- **Learn for one real task.** Describe your situation precisely and focus on the subtopics it needs.

Choose a topic and do one section tonight. Then do one tomorrow.
