At 4:40 on a Thursday in October, the fourteenth approval form of the week landed back on Helena's desk with a red circle around the signature box. Her company had shared a new purchase-approval policy a month earlier, and everyone had clicked "read". What it lacked was a learning readiness layer: the step between "policy shared" and "people ready".

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

The next morning Konrad, the finance director, gave her a deadline. "Internal audit reviews approvals in three weeks. I don't want to know who got the email. I want to know who understands the process."

## Before: a policy everyone had read

Helena is an operations director at a mid-sized distribution company. She, her colleagues and the company are fictional, but the gap will sound familiar. Finance, operations and procurement each touched the approval process differently, and each got a different part wrong: the wrong spending threshold, a missing second signature, "urgent" orders that skipped everyone.

Another all-staff email would only produce another round of clicks. So she chose one process, one group and three weeks, and decided to compare the before with the after.

## Week one: building a learning readiness layer from one policy

On Monday she exported the policy from Word to PDF and uploaded it to [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai). LearningClarity built a topic with subtopics: spending thresholds, who signs what, emergency purchases, finance checks.

She shared the topic by email with three team leads and the week's new hire. Each accepted and got their own copy with their own progress. She also sent each of them a Supervision request; once they accepted, a weekly email report would show her their topic progress, quizzes and exams.

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

The new hire was Tomasz, a procurement coordinator in his first week. Helena sketched the approval flow for him on paper, then left him with Explain Mode. When the section on split purchases felt dense, he tapped Simplify. On the signing chain he tapped Visualize and got a labelled diagram that matched her sketch, box for box.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

On Tuesday she briefed the leads: Adaeze from finance, Ravi from operations, Mei from procurement. "Same policy, different subtopics," she said. "Focus on the parts you touch. And in Quiz Mode, when you're unsure, press 'I don't know'. A guess tells me nothing."

## Week two: the result that stung

The first Friday reports were encouraging until Helena reached Ravi's. His "I don't know" answers clustered on one subtopic, emergency purchases, and his Exam Mode run, 20 untimed questions at Medium difficulty, ended with an AI performance summary that named the same area to revisit.

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

She booked a coffee with him, not a warning. Ravi was blunt. "When a forklift part breaks at six in the morning, I can't wait two days for a signature. The policy says urgent purchases follow the exception route. It never says what the route is."

He was right. The weak result had found a gap in the policy, not only in Ravi. Helena rewrote that section the same afternoon, built a fresh topic from the corrected PDF and shared it again. Ravi worked through the new section in Explain Mode, used Listen on the walk between loading bays, and retook the exam before the week was out with a clearly stronger result.

## After: the same Thursday, three weeks later

At 4:40 on the third Thursday, Helena looked at the same tray. Two forms had come back, both from a team outside the pilot. The reports showed the group's mastery rising on every subtopic, and Tomasz had pushed his first purchase order through the whole signing chain without a return.

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

The fewer returns were the team's own work: a clearer policy, an honest conversation and steady practice. What changed for Helena was that she could see the gaps before the auditors did.

Konrad read her one-page summary and handed it back. "First time I've seen the process and the evidence in the same document. Do expenses next."

## What the two Thursdays taught

- **Shared is not learned.** A read receipt shows access, not understanding.
- **"I don't know" is data.** Honest answers point straight at the weak subtopic.
- **A weak result can expose the content.** Sometimes the fix belongs in the policy, not the person.
