Late on a Friday afternoon the launch briefing at Halden Home was over, and Dario stood alone in the training room. Handouts lay on the chairs: plenty of training, no proof of training readiness. All 38 sales and support staff had attended and received the slides.

Then the COO leaned in at the door. "Launch is Monday after next. By Sunday night I want a list of who is ready to talk to customers about the new heating controller, by name. Not who attended. Who is ready."

Ten days, and Dario could not answer her.

## Ten days to launch: attended is not ready

Dario leads learning and development at Halden Home. He, his colleagues and the company are fictional, but every rollout team knows his problem. His reports measured completion: files opened, sessions attended. None of it measured training readiness: does a person understand the product well enough to help a real customer?

Another deck would add volume, not understanding.

On Monday morning he stood behind Zainab, a new support agent whose onboarding module on the current controller was marked complete. A customer asked when a fault should go to a field engineer. Zainab's hand hovered over the keyboard. She had finished the module. She did not have the answer.

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

Dario wrote in his notebook: "Complete is a decision about evidence, not about files."

## Six days out: define "complete" first

At sunrise on Tuesday he sorted the launch material into four piles: positioning, features, common customer questions and escalation rules. He exported the deck to PDF and uploaded it with the spec sheet to [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai), which built a topic and subtopics from them.

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

Before sharing anything, he defined "complete": every subtopic studied, a 30-question exam on the whole topic, 80% (the team's own bar), and a coaching conversation for anyone below it. Then he shared the topic with each colleague by email. Each accepted, got their own copy and progress, and invited Dario as a supervisor, so the email reports came straight to him.

## Five days out: practice, and a wobble

On Wednesday the teams worked through **Explain Mode**, one short section at a time. Anders, a support veteran, found the warranty section dense. Dario showed him **Simplify**, then **Visualize**, which drew a labelled diagram of the escalation path.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

That afternoon everyone took **Quiz Mode** on their hardest subtopics, with one rule: press "I don't know" rather than guess. (Sofia's story about [practice quizzes that hide what you don't know](/en/2026/10/practice-quizzes-stop-guessing/) shows why that honesty matters.)

On Thursday morning the reports stung. Almost everyone had answered "I don't know" on the same escalation questions. This was not 38 weak learners. It was one unclear page in Dario's own spec sheet. He rewrote it with the product team, walked everyone through it in fifteen minutes, and the exams ran that afternoon.

## Four days out: who needs coaching?

At dusk Dario sat with Margit, the sales manager, going through the **Exam Mode** results: scores, a full question review and an AI performance summary of strengths and areas to revisit. Everyone except Kofi had cleared 80%. Kofi, a new sales rep, had 64%, and his weak spot was escalation.

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

"So we coach him," Margit said. "We don't drop him from the launch."

On Friday afternoon Dario and Kofi sat in a quiet booth and went through the questions Kofi had missed, and why. That evening Kofi opened **Talk to This** and rehearsed customer questions out loud in several short calls, the tutor answering aloud.

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

On Saturday he retook the exam and scored 86%, which also earned him a certificate for the topic; Dario, as his supervisor, was notified. On Sunday night the COO had her list: 38 names.

## Launch day: the list meets the floor

On Monday the first calls came in. A customer asked when a faulty controller goes to an engineer, and Zainab answered in one calm sentence. An hour later Kofi handled the same question on a sales call. The answers were their own; the training made sure they had the knowledge behind them.

At noon Margit stopped by. "For once," she said, "the list matched the floor."

## Three takeaways for training readiness

- **Define "complete" before you share anything.** Name the subtopics, the result and the next step.
- **Read "I don't know" as information.** When everyone misses the same question, fix the material first.
- **Treat a low score as a coaching cue.** A conversation and a retake beat quiet exclusion.
