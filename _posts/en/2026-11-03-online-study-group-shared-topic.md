The group chat had 214 unread messages and not one useful summary. Ines scrolled through memes, lecture rumours and three different versions of "who has the slides?" on a Sunday night, and felt her stomach drop. Their online study group was supposed to get four second-year engineering students through the thermodynamics progress test in four weeks. Their lecturer had been clear: fail it, and you sit the resit in August.

{% include scene.html n=1 %}

The four of them were friends, but not at the same level. Ines was comfortable with the first law. Kwame was quick but careless. Marta had missed two weeks with a part-time job. And Bram, who had nearly switched courses in first year, felt furthest behind of all. (Ines, Bram, Kwame, Marta and their university are fictional.)

## Ines: one topic for an online study group

Another shared folder of fifty files would not help. Ines wanted one thing they could all study from. She exported the lecturer's slide deck to PDF, because PowerPoint decks can't be uploaded directly, and uploaded it to [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai). It built a topic from the slides, with subtopics for each part of the module.

Then she used **Sharing**. She typed each friend's email and sent the topic. Bram and Kwame previewed it and accepted, and a notification told her each time. Marta's share stayed pending: Ines had used her old address. She cancelled the pending share and sent it again. Because shared copies cannot be shared onward, she shared it with each of them herself.

## Bram: the friend who felt behind

On the tram on Monday morning, Bram opened his copy. It was independent, with his own progress, so nobody would see how slowly he started. He studied a subtopic in **Explain Mode**, one short section at a time, and tapped **Simplify** twice on entropy.

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

Then he took a **Quiz Mode** quiz on the subtopics he had chosen. He made himself press **"I don't know"** instead of guessing. The results showed more of those than he liked.

## Week one: the call that went nowhere

Their first Thursday call lasted ninety minutes. Everyone said they had "done some of it". Kwame told a story about his flatmate, Marta's tea went cold, and nobody could explain anything without notes. Bram stayed quiet. It was, Ines admitted, the group chat with faces.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

So she changed the rule. Learn alone, then teach each other. Before each call, everyone quizzed on their own and brought the subtopic with the most "I don't know" answers. On the call, each person had five minutes to explain it, then took questions.

## Week three: one focused hour

Bram's hardest subtopic was the second law. In Quiz Mode he asked why each wrong answer was wrong, worked through the sections again and drew the piston in his notebook until he could explain it without looking.

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

On the call he held the sketch up to the camera and talked for five minutes. Kwame asked a hard question. Bram answered it.

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

"That's the first time it made sense to me," Marta said. The call ended after one hour, and nobody minded.

The night before the test, Bram's mastery level on every subtopic was higher than in week one. The test result was his own work. But for the first time he walked into the hall knowing which parts he understood. (More on [why honest practice quizzes beat guessing](/en/2026/10/practice-quizzes-stop-guessing/).)

{% include scene.html n=6 %}

Outside, he opened the chat. "Passed. Same time Thursday for the next module?" Three thumbs up arrived in under a minute.

## Three takeaways

- **Learn alone, then teach each other.** Your own progress first, the group's hour second.
- **Share one source, not fifty files.** The same material keeps everyone on the same page.
- **Bring your "I don't know" to the group.** Your weakest subtopic is the best five minutes you can offer.

Share a topic you've built with one study partner this week, and agree on the subtopic each of you will explain.
