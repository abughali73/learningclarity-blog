"Explain, in your own words, why the steel goes at the bottom of the beam."

Dr. Okafor wrote the question on the whiteboard at 9:10 on a Monday morning and underlined it twice. "Friday week, in the tutorial, I'll ask some of you this out loud. No notes. If you can't answer it, you don't understand reinforced concrete yet, and the January exam will show it."

Layla, a second-year civil engineering student, copied the sentence carefully. She understood every word on the slide and still didn't understand the slide. She had grown up speaking Arabic and had studied in English since September. In lectures she could follow the words, but the idea underneath kept slipping away. (Layla, Dr. Okafor and their university are fictional.)

Rereading the slides gave her the same words again. Translating line by line on her phone gave her word salad. She needed the idea, not more vocabulary.

## Study in your native language first

That evening she exported the lecture deck to PDF, because PowerPoint decks can't be uploaded directly, and uploaded it to [the LearningClarity study platform](https://www.learningclarity.ai). Before creating the topic, she changed the learning language from English to Arabic. Her slides stayed in English. The explanations, quizzes and diagrams would come in Arabic.

She opened the first subtopic in **Explain Mode**. It taught one short section at a time, in formal standard Arabic rather than her family's dialect, but close enough to home. When the section on tension and compression felt heavy, she tapped **Simplify**. Then she typed her own question: "Why can't the concrete hold the tension itself?"

{% include scene.html n=2 %}

The answer landed in the language she thinks in. Concrete is strong when squeezed and weak when pulled. In a beam that bends under load, the top is squeezed and the bottom is pulled, so the steel goes where the pulling is. She tapped **Visualize** and got a clean, labelled diagram of the bending beam, labels in Arabic. She copied it into her notebook and sat back. The slide finally meant something.

## Wednesday: the quiz that stung

But the tutorial would be in English, and so would the exam. So she created a second topic from the same PDF, this time in English, and took a **Quiz Mode** quiz on the bending subtopics. She promised herself to press **"I don't know"** instead of guessing.

The score was lower than she expected. The results showed several wrong answers and a cluster of "I don't know", all on the neutral axis and tensile stress. She knew the ideas. She didn't own them in English yet. She asked why each wrong answer was wrong, and the English explanations tied the terms to what she had learned in Arabic.

{% include scene.html n=3 %}

She repeated the quiz on Friday and again on Sunday. The "I don't know" column shrank, and her mastery level on the English topic rose. (When an exam gets closer, here is [how one student practised for a hard timed exam](/en/2026/10/timed-exam-practice-accounting-student/).)

## Saying it out loud, in English

Reading an answer is not the same as saying one. On the walk home along the canal, she opened **Talk to This** on the English topic. The tutor replies aloud in the topic's language, so every conversation stayed in English. She kept each call short: explain the beam, take a follow-up question, stop. One on Monday, one on Tuesday, one on Thursday night, and she kept the transcripts to spot the words that still tripped her up.

{% include scene.html n=4 %}

## Friday, 10:15: the one hard question

"Layla," Dr. Okafor said. "Why does the steel go at the bottom?"

She walked to the whiteboard and drew a beam and two arrows. "When the beam carries a load, it bends. The top is in compression and the bottom is in tension. Concrete is strong in compression but cracks in tension, so the steel goes where the tension is." She paused. "On a cantilever the tension is at the top, so the steel moves up."

Dr. Okafor nodded slowly. "That last part wasn't on the slide. Say it like that in January."

{% include scene.html n=5 %}

The answer was hers: her notes, her practice, her nerve. She had simply learned the idea in Arabic first and practised it in English after.

## Three takeaways

- **Understand in your strongest language.** Concepts land faster in the language you think in.
- **Practise in the language of your exam.** A second topic turns understanding into words you can use.
- **Say it before you're asked.** Short spoken rehearsals expose the words that still trip you up.

Pick one lecture you only half understood, upload its slides, and choose the language you think best in before you create the topic.
